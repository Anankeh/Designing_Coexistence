01 · the problem

Six buildings, one quiet rule: every family for itself.

A community of 528 homes across six buildings holds a couple of thousand residents of many nationalities, some of them new to Spanish, so the house rules reached people unevenly. The administration spent its days answering complaints one at a time.

The daily friction
🔊Noise: footsteps, late parties, slammed doors, a neighbor's endless drilling.
🐾Pets: dogs barking for hours, animals loose in the halls, waste left behind.
🚗Parking: one paid space and three cars, visitor spots taken for good.
🧺Shared spaces: pool, gym, elevator and trash room left a mess, laundry over the facades.
💸Fees: a steady trickle of late and unpaid dues underneath it all.

Observation, interviews, and the complaint records pointed to one surprising cause. Most residents simply did not know the official rules. The behavior came from missing information, and a fine treats the symptom while the cause stays in place.

02 · the proposal

So we drew a zoo.

The campaign spoke a language the community already spoke. These animals live in everyday Colombian speech, so the meaning was already in everyone's head. A friendly visit from the right animal came first, a gentle paper reminder well ahead of any formal step.

03 · why these animals

Each character was already in their vocabulary.

Every animal was chosen because residents already used it to describe a behavior. Recognition did the work, because people saw themselves in a character they found familiar, and forgivable.

🐢
Turtle
Parking, and the patience it wore thin.
A turtle is everyday shorthand for slow. The label fit twice over, because clearing a badly parked car was its own slow ordeal: call security, track down the owner, and wait while the minutes stacked up.
🐦
Magpie
Noise, from raised voices to a television turned all the way up.
In Colombia an urraca is already what people call a loud neighbor, a word traded back and forth in everyday teasing, so the bird landed the moment it appeared.
🫏
Donkey
Carelessness, forgotten duties, and clumsiness around shared spaces.
Calling someone a burro already says undisciplined and forgetful, so the character named the habit gently and kept the lecture out of it.
🦍
Gorilla
Aggression and disrespect, most of it aimed at the cleaning and security staff.
The gorilla is the one who acts bigger than everyone else and leans on the people with the least power to answer back, and naming that made it visible.
🐰
Rabbit
Late and unpaid monthly fees.
To "hacer conejo," to do the rabbit, is a common local expression for slipping away without paying, so residents knew exactly what the rabbit meant.

The honest remainder
The portion that stayed asked for a different kind of help. Some residents were living with a health condition or an addiction, some were frequent travelers who had been away while the rules were updated, and a few situations had been raised quietly by their own families. Surfacing this group is the quiet value of the number, because once they were visible they could be guided toward specialized, confidential support. The rules reached everyone, the campaign eased most of the friction, and this first rollout left clear room to keep lowering the figure over time.

The deepest change was cultural. The community moved toward shared responsibility, and what began as an institutional proposal became a shared norm, which is where behavior change becomes permanent.

A financial loop helped. An early-payment discount lifted compliance and rebuilt a sense of fairness, and the recovered money went visibly back into the building, into better lighting and cleaner common areas people could see.

"Nobody likes a rule until they live with it and feel that it protects them."

The method
Friendly Compliance, reframing a rule as collective care, using sympathetic, culturally-native characters and humor to lower defensiveness.
